## Training for a reliable working partner

Service dog training goes beyond basic obedience. A working dog must be able to behave reliably in public environments, remain focused around distractions and perform the skills or tasks required by its handler.

## How service dog training works

Each team's plan is built around the handler, but the training follows the same order.

1. **Consultation and evaluation.** We learn what you need help with, then evaluate your dog, or help you choose one, for the temperament service work demands.
2. **Foundation obedience.** Reliable sit, down, stay, come, loose-leash walking and settling on a mat, first at home and then around distractions.
3. **Public access behavior.** Calm, focused behavior in stores, restaurants, waiting rooms and parking lots: ignoring dropped food, strangers and other dogs, and settling quietly under a table.
4. **Task training.** The specific tasks that help with your disability, taught in small steps and practiced until they're dependable.
5. **Proofing and practice.** Tasks rehearsed in the places your team goes, plus the ongoing practice that keeps a working dog sharp.

Public access and task training often overlap. How long each stage takes depends on the dog, the tasks and how consistently the team practices. We use positive reinforcement within a balanced approach, building trust and motivation rather than relying on punishment.

Training starts in your home and moves into the places you actually go: your grocery store, your pharmacy, your doctor's office, downtown Wilmington along the Riverwalk.

## Ways to get a service dog

**Train the dog you already have.** We start with an evaluation. A service dog needs to stay calm in crowds, be neutral toward strangers and other dogs, recover quickly from surprises and be physically suited to its tasks. Plenty of wonderful family dogs aren't a fit for public work, and we'll tell you honestly where your dog stands.

**Start with a puppy chosen for the job.** We can help you select a puppy from an established breeder, keep an eye on its temperament as it grows and handle its early socialization and obedience. The goal is a puppy with solid obedience by about six months old, ready to begin advanced public access and task work. You're involved from the start, so your bond grows along with the training. Our [puppy training](/puppy-training/) page covers those early foundations.

## Therapy dog training

A therapy dog brings comfort to many people, in hospitals, nursing homes, schools and community centers, rather than performing tasks for one handler. Therapy dog training is for friendly, steady dogs whose owners want to share them with others. Your dog should already follow commands reliably and enjoy meeting new people. Young puppies usually aren't ready, so most dogs start once they've matured and finished obedience training.

Training covers the manners a visit demands: loose-leash walking through busy hallways, polite greetings without jumping, a dependable "leave it" and calm behavior around wheelchairs, walkers and sudden noises. After training, you apply for certification through a therapy dog organization, and regular practice keeps your dog ready for visits. Therapy dogs don't have public access rights under the ADA. They visit where they're invited.

For more on how service dogs, therapy dogs and emotional support animals differ, read our [guide to all three](/resources/service-dog-and-therapy-dog-training-wilmington/).

## Service dog laws in North Carolina

A quick summary of the rules our clients ask about most. This is general information, not legal advice.

- **What counts as a service dog.** The Americans with Disabilities Act defines a service animal as a dog individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. Dogs whose only job is comfort or emotional support don't qualify.
- **No certification required.** The ADA doesn't require certification, registration, an ID card or a vest. Certificates and registrations sold online don't grant any rights under the ADA.
- **What a business can ask.** When it isn't obvious what a service dog does, staff may ask only whether the dog is required because of a disability and what work or task it has been trained to perform.
- **Owner training is allowed.** The ADA doesn't require a professional training program. People with disabilities may train their own dogs, and many work with a trainer to reach the reliability public access demands.
- **Control in public.** A service dog must be harnessed, leashed or tethered unless that would interfere with its work or the handler's disability prevents it, and the handler must keep the dog under control.
- **Dogs in training.** The ADA covers only dogs that are already trained. North Carolina law ([G.S. 168-4.2](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_168/GS_168-4.2.html)) allows an animal in training to become a service animal to enter public places for training when it's accompanied by the person training it and wears a collar and leash, harness or cape identifying it as a service animal in training.
- **North Carolina registration.** The NC Department of Health and Human Services issues free service animal registrations under [G.S. 168-4.3](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_168/GS_168-4.3.html). Registration is voluntary, and the ADA doesn't require it.

The U.S. Department of Justice answers more questions in its [service animal FAQ](https://www.ada.gov/resources/service-animals-faqs/).
