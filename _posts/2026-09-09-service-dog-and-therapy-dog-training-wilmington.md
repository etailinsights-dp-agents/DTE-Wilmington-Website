These three terms get mixed up constantly, and the differences matter, legally and practically. Here is the plain-English version we give Wilmington families, followed by how the training actually works.

## Service dog, therapy dog, or emotional support animal?

**A service dog** is individually trained to perform specific tasks for one person with a disability: guiding, alerting, retrieving medication, interrupting an episode, providing stability. Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, service dogs have public access rights, meaning they can accompany their handler in places pets cannot go. The bar for their training and behavior is correspondingly high.

**A therapy dog** works with many people rather than one handler. Therapy dog teams visit hospitals, schools, and care facilities to provide comfort. They do not have ADA public access rights; they go where they are invited, and most facilities require certification through a therapy dog organization.

**An emotional support animal** provides comfort to its owner by presence alone. No task training is required, and ESAs do not have public access rights. A vest from the internet does not change any of this, and misrepresenting a pet as a service dog undermines the teams who depend on the real thing.

## How service dog training works here

Real service dog work is a long, staged process, and it starts in the same place all good training starts: rock-solid obedience. Before a dog can alert to anything, it has to be reliable around distractions, neutral toward strangers and other dogs, and steady in public spaces. From that foundation, training moves to the specific tasks that serve the handler's needs, and then to proofing those tasks everywhere the team will go.

## Therapy dog training

For therapy work, the training centers on bomb-proof calm: loose-leash walking through crowds, gentle greetings with strangers of every age, indifference to wheelchairs, walkers, dropped food, and sudden noises.
